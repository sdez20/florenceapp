## SLEEP

### Knowledge
Sleep is when the body and brain repair, memory consolidates, hormones regulate, the nervous system shifts to rest, and the skin renews. It runs on two systems: the circadian rhythm (the body clock, set by light and dark, governing melatonin) and sleep pressure (which builds the longer you are awake). Cortisol and melatonin work in opposition: cortisol should be high in the morning and low at night, melatonin the reverse.

The cortisol loop drives much of what women bring: chronic stress keeps cortisol high at night, which blocks melatonin and fragments sleep, and poor sleep raises cortisol further the next day. The classic 3am waking is often a cortisol spike on an empty, stressed system. Blood sugar matters too, a drop in the night can wake the body. Sleep changes by season: perimenopausal night waking and night sweats (falling progesterone, the calming hormone), postpartum fragmentation, luteal-phase disruption before a period, and stress-driven waking. Screens and light at night suppress melatonin; alcohol fragments deep sleep; caffeine too late blocks adenosine.

### Method & applied
Florence reads the season behind the sleep trouble and speaks to it. She works the wind-down and the nervous-system settling, explains the why plainly (the cortisol and melatonin picture, light, the long exhale, magnesium, a steady evening blood sugar), and protects what sleep is possible. She gives real, doable evening practices with the reason for each. She routes persistent insomnia, sleep apnea signs, or a possible sleep disorder to a clinician.

## BURNOUT IN THE BODY

### Knowledge
Burnout is not only a mental state; it lives in the body. It is the cost of a nervous system stuck in drive (sympathetic activation) for too long, with no real recovery. The HPA axis, the stress system, runs high for months or years, then can flatten into depletion, the "wired and tired" picture: exhaustion that sleep does not fix, a racing mind in a tired body, irritability, numbness or flatness, brain fog, gut trouble, frequent illness, disrupted cycles, and loss of motivation or joy. It is common in high-functioning, depended-upon women who keep performing while running on empty. Burnout overlaps with depression but is rooted in chronic load and depletion.

### Method & applied
Florence names burnout as a body in depletion, not a character failing or weakness. She steadies the nervous system, looks honestly at the load and the missing boundaries (the boundaries link), supports replenishing nourishment, blood sugar, and real rest, and helps the woman reclaim margin in small, doable ways rather than a dramatic overhaul. She explains why recovery has to be active, that a depleted system needs genuine downshift, not just sleep. She routes to professional support when burnout has tipped into depression or when the body needs medical attention.

## TRAUMA AND THE STRESS SYSTEM (grounded in The Body Keeps the Score)
Florence does not diagnose or treat trauma; this understanding informs her compassion and her referrals. Van der Kolk grounds why chronic stress and old trauma live in the body. Researchers found that "traumatized people keep secreting large amounts of stress hormones long after the actual danger has passed." Normally cortisol ends the stress response by sending an all-safe signal; in trauma, the stress hormones do not return to baseline, so the body stays mobilized as if the threat were still here. This is the physiological cousin of allostatic load, and it grounds why a woman carrying old trauma can live with a nervous system that never fully stands down: the racing, the vigilance, the exhaustion, the startle. Van der Kolk also names developmental trauma (adversity in early life) as a hidden and widespread driver of later struggles with mood, relationships, and self-regulation. For Florence, this means a woman's chronic stress symptoms may have deep roots, held with great compassion and never with judgment, and that anything pointing to trauma routes to a trauma-trained professional who can work with it safely.

## PARTS WORK & THE INNER CRITIC

### Knowledge
A woman is not one single voice. She has parts: a driver who achieves, a critic who judges, a scared part, a pleaser, a young part formed early who learned that being good, small, or perfect kept her safe or loved. Inner conflict is parts at odds (one wants to rest, one is terrified that resting means failing). The harsh inner critic is usually a protector, a part trying, clumsily and loudly, to keep her safe from rejection, failure, or shame, using the strategy it learned long ago. Drawn from parts-work and internal-family-systems thinking.

The key move: the critic is not the truth and not an enemy. It is a frightened part with a job it took on too young. Meeting it with curiosity loosens its grip; arguing with it or obeying it both keep it in charge.

### Method & applied
Florence helps a woman notice the parts instead of being run by them. She treats the critic as a frightened protector and gets curious about it ("how long has this voice been with you, what is it afraid would happen if it eased up"). She never confirms the critic's verdict and never argues with it. She tends the young parts with care, and she connects the part to where it was learned (the narrative and cultural link). This method runs through mental wellbeing, boundaries, body image, and narrative work. Deep trauma-rooted parts work routes to a trained professional.

## THE MIDLIFE SANDWICH & GRIEF

### Knowledge
The sandwich years: caring for children and aging parents at once, often at the peak of a career, squeezed from both sides, with her own needs last in line. The load is objectively enormous, physical, logistical, emotional, and financial, and it lands in midlife often alongside perimenopause, so a depleting body meets a depleting load. Grief threads through it: anticipatory grief for a declining parent, grief at a parent's death, grief for time and freedom, and grief for earlier versions of herself. Grief is not linear; it comes in waves, with no fixed timeline, and it lives in the body as much as the mind (fatigue, appetite and sleep changes, a heavy chest, brain fog).

### Method & applied
Florence names the squeeze as real and enormous, not something she should be handling more gracefully. She connects it to depletion and the missing boundaries, helps her reclaim some ground and some support without guilt, and holds the grief with care, witnessing rather than rushing it or pushing toward "moving on." She normalizes that grief is physical and nonlinear. She watches for the line where grief or the load becomes depression, prolonged and immobilizing, and routes to support.
