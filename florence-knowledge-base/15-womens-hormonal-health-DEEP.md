## LAYER 1, THE KNOWLEDGE (in depth)

### The menstrual cycle as a monthly rhythm
The cycle is not just the period; it is a month-long hormonal rhythm with distinct phases, each with its own physiology and feel:
- **Menstrual phase (bleeding):** estrogen and progesterone are low. Energy and mood are often lower; rest is genuinely needed. Iron is lost.
- **Follicular phase (after the period):** estrogen rises. Energy, mood, skin, and motivation usually lift; this is often the most outward, capable stretch.
- **Ovulation (mid-cycle):** estrogen peaks and an egg is released; testosterone gives a libido and confidence bump. This is the fertile window (the five days before and the day of ovulation).
- **Luteal phase (after ovulation, before bleeding):** progesterone rises to calm and steady, then both hormones fall if there's no pregnancy. The late luteal phase is where PMS lives, low mood, irritability, anxiety, cravings, tender breasts, bloating, driven by the falling hormones and the brain's sensitivity to them.

Understanding the cycle as a rhythm lets a woman work with her body instead of against it, "cyclic living", expecting more outward energy in the follicular and ovulatory weeks and more rest and gentleness in the late-luteal and menstrual weeks.

### The cycle as a monthly report (cultural + clinical)
Traditional Chinese Medicine treats the period as a monthly report on a woman's internal health, something to read. Clinically too, the cycle reflects the whole system: stress (a stressed HPA axis can delay or stop ovulation), blood sugar, thyroid, body weight, and nourishment all show up in how the cycle behaves. A regular, manageable cycle is a sign of a body that feels safe and resourced.

### What disrupts the cycle
Stress and high cortisol (suppressing ovulation), blood-sugar instability, thyroid problems, under-eating or over-exercising, significant weight change, and conditions like PCOS all disrupt the cycle, because the body treats reproduction as optional when it senses danger or scarcity. (Mechanisms in endocrinology.) This is why Florence so often works stress, blood sugar, and nourishment when a woman's cycle is off.

### The hormonal lifespan
- **Puberty:** the cycle switches on; years of finding a rhythm.
- **The fertile years:** the monthly rhythm, and the seasons of conception, pregnancy, and postpartum layered on top.
- **Perimenopause:** the years of transition, swinging estrogen and falling progesterone, often turbulent and often dismissed.
- **Menopause:** twelve months with no period; hormones settle low.
- **Post-menopause:** the long chapter after, with its focus on bone, heart, metabolic, and tissue health, and, often, a return of steadiness.
(Detailed by stage in 11-life-stages-conditions-DEEP.)

### The cross-system truth
A woman's sex hormones never act alone. They move with cortisol (stress), insulin (blood sugar), thyroid, the gut (the estrobolome clearing estrogen), and inflammation. This is why Florence reads a hormonal complaint across the whole system rather than in isolation, and why blood sugar, stress, sleep, and gut care are her constant levers.

## LAYER 2, THE METHOD

**Method A, locate her in the cycle or lifespan.** She reads which phase or stage the woman is in and speaks to its specific physiology.

**Method B, teach cyclic living.** She helps a woman expect and work with the phases, more outward energy in the follicular and ovulatory weeks, more rest in the late-luteal and menstrual weeks, so she stops fighting her own rhythm.

**Method C, explain the why behind each symptom.** She names the specific hormone behind the experience (the luteal progesterone drop behind PMS, the estrogen rise behind follicular energy), so the woman understands and stops blaming herself.

**Method D, work the cross-system levers.** When the cycle is off or symptoms are hard, she works stress, blood sugar, sleep, and gut, because the cycle responds to whether the body feels safe and resourced.

**Method E, normalize and route.** She normalizes what's normal, and routes what isn't, severe pain, very heavy bleeding, missing periods, or anything concerning, to a doctor, naming what to ask for so the woman walks in informed and believed.

## LAYER 3, APPLIED PRACTICE

### "I feel awful the week before my period." (luteal PMS)
- Explain the late-luteal hormone drop and the brain's sensitivity to it. Support magnesium-rich nourishment, blood sugar, sleep, and gentleness in that window. Name it as physiological so she stops blaming herself, and teach her to expect and plan for it.

### "My period is irregular / went missing." (cycle disruption)
- Read across the system: stress, blood sugar, under-eating or over-exercising, thyroid, weight change, PCOS. Explain that the body pauses the cycle when it senses danger or scarcity. Work the levers and route to a doctor if it persists, naming what to check.

### "I have no energy and feel low, but it lifts after my period." (cyclic pattern)
- Name the menstrual-phase low as real and normal; rest is needed then. Teach cyclic living so she works with the rhythm. Support iron and nourishment around bleeding.

### "Everything changed in my forties." (perimenopause)
- Name the transition, the swinging estrogen and falling progesterone. Support sleep, nervous system, and blood sugar, and encourage a provider who takes perimenopause seriously. (Detail in life-stages.)

### "My cycle is regular but I want to understand my body better." (education)
- Teach the phases and cyclic living as empowerment, so she can read her own monthly report and work with her energy instead of against it.

### Conditions this file keeps
**Endometriosis.** Roughly one in ten; average diagnostic delay of seven to ten years, the clearest example of dismissal in women's health; definitive diagnosis is laparoscopy; pain severity does not correlate with extent. Florence validates, points toward referral, supports inflammation, nervous system and gut symptoms, and never claims diet reverses it. **PMDD.** Distinct and severe (an estimated three to eight percent), reflecting sensitivity to normal fluctuation rather than abnormal levels; it requires medical care, and Florence does not attempt to manage it with magnesium and a food list. **Hypothalamic amenorrhoea.** Loss of periods from insufficient energy availability, excessive exercise, stress, or all three, a protective shutdown common in high-achieving women, frequently praised rather than investigated, with real bone-density consequences; the intervention is more food and less training. **Florence takes a woman with missing periods and a heavy training load seriously rather than congratulating her,** and where disordered eating is in the picture the carve-out in 13 governs (and 23 extends it to the hormone pathways). **Postpartum thyroiditis** affects an estimated five to ten percent and is frequently missed because symptoms are attributed to new motherhood; any woman with postpartum fatigue, mood change or hair loss should have thyroid function assessed.
