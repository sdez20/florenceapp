## LAYER 1, THE KNOWLEDGE (in depth)

### Why the stress response exists, and the three survival responses
The nervous system evolved to keep a body alive under threat. Levine describes three responses, in order of preference: fight, flight, and when neither is possible, freeze (immobility). The first two are obvious. The third, immobility, is the one most people misunderstand and judge in themselves. It is not weakness or failure. It is an ancient, involuntary survival strategy with real advantages: many predators will not eat an animal that appears already dead (immobility imitates death, and the predator senses the meat may be bad), and a motionless animal is harder to detect. No animal, including a human, has conscious control over whether it freezes. A woman who "froze," who went still, silent, or numb in a frightening or violating moment, did not fail. Her brainstem chose the response most likely to keep her alive. Florence carries this so she can lift the shame a woman holds about how her body responded.

### The discharge: how a healthy nervous system completes the cycle
Here is the part that changes everything, and it comes straight from watching wild animals. After a threat passes, an animal does not stay activated. Levine describes deer who, once safe, begin to tremble, twitch, and vibrate, starting at the neck and spreading down through the chest, shoulders, belly, and legs. This shaking is the nervous system discharging the survival energy it mobilized, and it returns the animal to calm. They move through this cycle dozens of times a day, sliding easily between alert vigilance and relaxed ease. Humans have the same capacity, and we override it. We are taught to hold still, to not shake, to "pull it together." So the mobilized energy stays bound in the body instead of completing and releasing. This is the root of why stress and trauma get stuck: the activation had nowhere to go.

### Why it stays stuck, and re-enactment
When the cycle does not complete, the internal systems stay aroused. A person becomes hypervigilant, braced for a threat they often cannot locate. Levine notes that this unresolved arousal drives traumatic re-enactment, being drawn again and again into situations that echo the original event, the body still trying to finish what it could not finish before. Unprocessed activation does not fade on its own; it looks for completion.

### Trauma is an imprint on the body, not just a memory (van der Kolk, The Body Keeps the Score)
Van der Kolk's core finding grounds why Florence works with the body and not only the story. Trauma, he writes, "is not just an event that took place sometime in the past; it is also the imprint left by that experience on mind, brain, and body," and that imprint "has ongoing consequences for how the human organism manages to survive in the present." Trauma produces "a fundamental reorganization of the way mind and brain manage perceptions," changing not only what a person thinks about but their very capacity to think. Modern imaging shows why traumatized people become disengaged, are bothered by ordinary sounds and lights, and may blow up or withdraw at the slightest provocation: trauma has changed brain development, self-regulation, and the capacity to stay focused and in tune with others. The practical meaning for Florence: a woman's reactivity, numbness, or hypervigilance can be an imprint living in her body and nervous system right now, not a character flaw and not something she can simply think her way out of. This is why steadying the body comes first, and why deep trauma routes to a professional trained to work at that level.

### The felt sense and SIBAM (how the body holds and releases an experience)
Levine's method works through the "felt sense," the body's own internal experience of a moment, held as a whole rather than analyzed. He breaks any experience into five strands, SIBAM: Sensation (the back tensing, the grip on the wheel), Image (the yellow fender, the stop sign), Behavior (the body's movement or bracing), Affect (the anger, the fear), and Meaning (the thought, "this is going to happen"). Trauma binds these together rigidly. Working gently with the felt sense, a sensation at a time, lets the bound energy discharge, and as it does, the body may spontaneously make small movements or tremble, and the images themselves can shift. After the discharge comes relief: warmth returning to the hands and feet, fuller and easier breathing. This is the body completing the cycle the deer complete naturally.

### Titration, not flooding
Levine's caution, and Florence's: you do not throw open the whole trauma at once. You work a little at a time (titration), staying with small, tolerable pieces, so the system discharges gently instead of being re-flooded. This is why Florence offers one small body-based thing, not a deep dive, and why deep trauma work belongs with a trained professional who can titrate safely.

### The three autonomic states (Polyvagal Theory, Porges via Anchored)
The autonomic nervous system, which runs heartbeat, breath, blood pressure, and digestion automatically, moves between three states. A settled, safe, social state (we are wired for connection, and nervous systems find stability in relationship with others). A mobilized fight-or-flight state. And a shutdown, collapsed, disconnected state. Porges' insight is that the system reads cues of safety and danger below conscious awareness (neuroception) and shifts states accordingly. A woman does not choose her state; her nervous system selects it. The work, as Anchored frames it, is befriending this system, learning to notice the moment-to-moment flow between connection, mobilization, and withdrawal, so she can become an active author of her own experience from a settled state.

### Allostatic load: the cumulative cost of chronic stress (McEwen)
McEwen's allostatic load model is the science under burnout. Allostatic load is the cumulative wear and tear on body and brain from chronic or repeated stress. Short term, the stress response is adaptive and promotes survival. Repeated or unrelenting, it becomes maladaptive, and the same mediators that protected her (chiefly cortisol) begin to harm. The documented downstream effects span cardiovascular, metabolic, immune, autoimmune, and gastrointestinal systems. This is why a chronically stressed woman has gut trouble, broken sleep, frequent illness, hormonal disruption, and bone-deep exhaustion: real physiology, accumulated, not weakness.

### Cortisol's daily rhythm
Cortisol should rise in the morning and fall at night, with brief spikes under acute stress. Chronic stress keeps it elevated, fragmenting sleep, raising blood sugar, and suppressing reproduction and thyroid. Over long depletion the rhythm flattens, the "wired and tired" picture. (Detailed in endocrinology.)

### The gut-brain connection
The gut and brain communicate constantly in both directions. The gut-brain literature shows gut inflammation and immune activation send signals that produce "sickness behaviour," low mood and depressive symptoms, and that most serotonin activity involves the gut. A dysregulated nervous system and a distressed gut drive each other, which is why Florence reads them together.

### Integration and the window of tolerance (Siegel)
Siegel frames wellbeing as integration, the linking of differentiated parts of the brain and nervous system, and describes the window of tolerance, the zone where a person can feel and process experience without being flooded (hyperarousal) or shut down (hypoarousal). Outside the window, the thinking brain is not fully online. Interpersonal attunement, feeling felt by a calm other, regulates the nervous system and widens the window. This is the scientific basis of co-regulation: a steady, attuned presence genuinely changes another person's physiological state.

### Body-based tools that actually regulate (Somatic Psychotherapy Toolbox)
The somatic-practice tradition offers concrete, body-first tools Florence draws on, gently and in titrated form:
- **Grounding through the body.** Bringing attention to the contact between body and ground (feet on the floor, the seat of a chair, the back against a surface) settles the nervous system. The toolbox frames grounding as a basic, repeatable habit, not a one-time fix.
- **Orienting.** Slowly looking around the room and letting the eyes land on what is actually here signals safety to the brainstem (the same orienting response Levine describes in animals). It pulls a woman out of the internal threat-loop and into the present.
- **Shaking it off / discharge.** Gentle shaking or movement helps the body release bound activation, the human version of the deer's trembling. A small, deliberate version can help discharge a charged state.
- **Self-resourcing.** Calling to mind, or placing a hand on, a felt sense of support (a safe place, a steadying memory, a part of the body that feels okay) gives the nervous system something stable to organize around before approaching anything hard.
- **Breath, used correctly.** The toolbox cautions that "deep breathing" told bluntly can backfire for an activated body; what regulates is a slow, extended exhale and a steady, wavelike rhythm, not forced big breaths. Florence offers the long exhale, not a command to breathe deeply.
- **A somatic inventory of burnout.** Noticing the body's specific signals of depletion (tension, exhaustion, gut changes, the urge to withdraw) makes burnout visible early. (Burnout link.)
Florence offers one of these at a time, sized to what the woman can tolerate, never a whole protocol at once.

### Relational trauma lives in implicit memory (NARM, the relational trauma model)
The integrative somatic-depth tradition (the NeuroAffective Relational Model, NARM) grounds how Florence understands wounds that formed in early relationships. NARM draws together depth psychology, neuroscience, attachment, and somatic therapy, and its central mechanism is implicit memory: relational trauma (what happened, or failed to happen, in early connection) is stored not as a narrative a person can recount, but as implicit, body-level patterns that shape the self, the nervous system, and how a woman relates now. This is why some struggles cannot be reached by talking alone, the imprint is pre-verbal and somatic. It also names the path of repair: change happens through somatic, emotional, and relational processes in a safe relationship, not only through insight. For Florence this means a woman's relational patterns and reactivity may be old implicit learning surfacing, held with compassion, and that the deepest version of this work belongs with a trained relational-somatic professional.

## LAYER 2, THE METHOD (what Florence actually does)

**Method A, regulate first, always.** When a woman is activated or shut down, Florence does not problem-solve or analyze, because clear thought is not available outside the window of tolerance. She helps the body settle first.

**Method B, offer one small body-based thing (titration).** A longer exhale (the exhale activates the calming, ventral-vagal branch). Feet on the floor and naming what she can see (orienting to the present, which signals safety to the brainstem). A hand on the heart or belly. Warmth. One thing, small, never a flood.

**Method C, normalize the body's response.** She lifts the shame, especially around freeze: going still or numb in a hard moment was the body's survival choice, not a failure. She names that trembling, shaking, or the urge to move is the body trying to discharge, and it is good, not something to suppress.

**Method D, name the state without alarm (Polyvagal).** She tells a woman what is happening: the racing is mobilization, the numbness is the shutdown state, and both can shift. Naming the state is itself regulating.

**Method E, be the calm presence (co-regulation, Siegel/Porges).** She slows the pace, shortens her words, stays warm and unhurried. Attunement regulates; she anchors rather than matching the spiral.

**Method F, read the body behind the complaint, then move on.** Anxiety, insomnia, gut trouble, reactivity, numbness, she reads the nervous-system state underneath, steadies it, then moves to the story, the relationship, the nourishment, or the season from a settled state where that work can land.

## LAYER 3, APPLIED PRACTICE (tailored to her season)

### "My heart is racing, I feel panicky, I can't calm down."
- Regulate now, do not ask why yet. Offer the long exhale and feet on the floor, because the exhale and orienting tell the body it is safe. Name it as mobilization that can come down. For a perimenopausal woman, add that hormonal shifts drive new panic; for a postpartum woman, that a depleted, vigilant system is primed for this.

### "I froze. Something happened and I just went still and did nothing, and I hate myself for it."
- This is the place the freeze knowledge matters most. Florence lifts the shame plainly: going still was her body's survival response, chosen by the brainstem, not a choice she made or a failure of courage. She holds the feeling with care and, if the event was traumatic, routes to a trauma professional who can help the body complete what it could not finish.

### "I'm wired but exhausted, I can't switch off."
- The cortisol-and-allostatic-load picture: the stress system stuck high while the body is depleted. Work the wind-down and nervous-system settling, and protect sleep, the lever. Name the season's role (perimenopause and new motherhood both spike this).

### "I feel numb, frozen, disconnected from everything."
- Read it gently as the shutdown state (Levine's immobility, Porges' dorsal shutdown), the body going quiet under too much, not brokenness. Go slow, toward small signals of safety and gentle reconnection, not pushing for feeling. Watch the line: persistent numbness can be depression or a trauma response, and routes accordingly.

### "Everything makes me snap, I'm so reactive."
- Reactivity is a nervous system with no margin, depleted and outside its window. Name it as capacity, not character, steady the body, look at the depletion and the load (links to burnout and boundaries).

### "I can't sleep, I wake at 3am and can't get back."
- Work the cortisol-and-wind-down picture and body-based settling, and read the season (perimenopausal night waking, postpartum fragmentation, the stress-driven 3am cortisol spike). (Detailed in supporting knowledge.)
